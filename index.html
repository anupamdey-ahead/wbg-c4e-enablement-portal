Reliability principles we use
SRE

Reliability is a product decision

Reliability is defined in terms of user experience and business impact, not component uptime, so trade-offs are made deliberately.

SRE

Manage reliability through measurable objectives

Service level objectives make reliability explicit, comparable, and reviewable rather than a matter of opinion.

Observability

Design observability with the workload

Instrumentation, metadata, and signals are planned alongside the service rather than retrofitted after an incident.

Observability

Correlate service context across the stack

Topology, traces, logs, and experience data are connected so impact analysis does not depend on manual correlation.

Operations

Safe change reduces risk

Progressive delivery, canaries, validation, and rollback paths limit the blast radius of every change.

Operations

Shared ownership keeps teams accountable

Product teams own their services; the C4E provides standards and support rather than taking that ownership over.

Google SRE

Spend the error budget on purpose

The gap between 100% and the SLO is a budget for risk — spend it deliberately on releases and experiments, and slow down when it runs out.

Azure Well-Architected

Design for failure, not just for success

Assume components will fail. Set recovery targets up front, and test the failure paths before you need them in production.

DORA

Fast and stable aren't in tension

Independent research consistently finds elite teams are both — small changes, fast feedback, and good rollback paths deliver speed and safety together, not one at the expense of the other.

SRE Fundamentals

A practical reference for the concepts the delivery team uses every day.

Getting the foundation right means coverage, metadata, and service context are correct before deeper capabilities are layered on — because tagging, naming, and topology decisions taken early determine whether dashboards, alerts, and impact analysis are usable later.

Core concepts

Objectives over uptimeReliability is expressed as measurable objectives, not component availability.
Context over signalsA signal is only useful when it carries the service context needed to act on it.
Ownership over handoverProduct teams own their services; the C4E owns standards and enablement.
Distributed tracingServices, spans, and tracesHow Dynatrace identifies services and captures the path of a request across them.

A service is a logical unit of work that Dynatrace detects from running processes. A span is a single timed operation within that work, and a trace is the connected set of spans that make up one request as it crosses services, queues, and databases. Together they turn a request into something you can reason about end to end.

  • Service detection is automatic, but detection and naming rules can be adjusted where defaults misrepresent the service.
  • Traces are what connect a user-facing symptom to the specific downstream component causing it.
  • Service naming and tagging decisions taken during deployment determine how usable traces are later.
InstrumentationTrace ingestion: OneAgent and OpenTelemetryTwo routes to distributed traces, with different operating trade-offs.

OneAgent and OpenTelemetry are two routes to the same outcome. OneAgent instruments automatically at the host level and requires no code change, which makes it the default for most services. OpenTelemetry requires the application to emit its own telemetry, which costs development effort but suits services that already standardise on it or run where an agent cannot be installed.

DecisionGuidance
The service runs on a host or cluster you controlUse OneAgent — automatic detection and no code change.
The service already emits OpenTelemetryIngest the existing OpenTelemetry data rather than duplicating instrumentation.
The runtime cannot host an agentUse OpenTelemetry export, and confirm the trace context survives every hop.

OpenTelemetry itself is vendor-neutral — it's a CNCF project, not a Dynatrace one. For the platform-agnostic view of what it is and isn't: opentelemetry.io ↗.

AvailabilitySynthetic monitoringHTTP and browser monitors that actively test critical journeys.

Synthetic monitoring runs scheduled HTTP or browser tests against a journey from outside the application, so availability and response time are measured whether or not real traffic is present. It is the only signal that reports a problem on a service nobody is currently using.

  • Choose monitors for the critical journeys agreed in Discovery, not for every endpoint.
  • A failing synthetic monitor with no real-user impact usually points at the monitor, not the service — review both before alerting on it.

The frameworks, browsable
Batch & processBatch and process observabilityWhy scheduled and asynchronous work needs its own signals, not just app and infra metrics.

Batch jobs, scheduled reports, and queue-driven processing fail differently than request-response services — a batch job doesn't "go down," it runs late, drops records, or silently produces a partial result. Left uninstrumented, these failures are usually caught late: by a reconciliation exception, a missing report, or a business user asking where their data is, rather than by an alert.

  • Treat job duration, completion status, and queue depth as first-class signals, not an afterthought bolted onto infrastructure monitoring.
  • Watch for dead-letter or retry-queue growth — a silently growing backlog is often the earliest sign something is wrong.
  • A batch chain is really a transaction path with a schedule instead of a request — map it end to end the same way you would a user journey (see D2).
Google SRESLOs and error budgetsThe Google SRE model for turning "how reliable should this be?" into a number you can act on.

An SLI is what you measure (say, the percentage of requests faster than 300ms). An SLO is the target you set for that SLI (99.5% of requests this fast, over 30 days). The gap between the SLO and 100% is the error budget — the amount of unreliability you've agreed is acceptable. As long as budget remains, teams can spend it on risk: shipping faster, running experiments, taking on planned maintenance. Once it's exhausted, the SRE model says to stop spending it — slow down releases and prioritize reliability work until the budget recovers.

  • An SLO is a decision, not a measurement — pick a target the business actually needs, not the highest number the platform can report.
  • The "four golden signals" — latency, traffic, errors, and saturation — are a good starting set of SLIs for most user-facing services. Full chapter: Monitoring Distributed Systems ↗.
  • Further reading: the SLOs chapter of Google's SRE book ↗, and the SRE Workbook's hands-on SLO chapter ↗ for worked examples.
Google SREToil, and why this hub existsThe Google SRE term for manual, repetitive work that grows with the service — and the case for standardizing onboarding.

Google's SRE book defines toil as work that's manual, repetitive, automatable, tactical, and — critically — scales linearly as the service or the estate grows. A one-off manual fix isn't toil. Re-running the same manual onboarding checklist for the two-hundredth service is. Toil doesn't just cost time; it crowds out the engineering work that would actually reduce future toil.

  • This is the direct case for the milestone guides in this hub: a documented, repeatable Key Steps procedure turns toil into a standard, and frees people to spend time on the judgment calls that actually need a person.
  • If a Key Steps line item feels like pure toil every time you run it, that's a signal to raise it as an improvement backlog item (see Executive Overview) rather than just repeating it.
  • Full detail, with real case studies of toil reduction: the SRE Workbook's Eliminating Toil chapter ↗.
Azure Well-ArchitectedThe Reliability pillarMicrosoft's five reliability design principles for cloud workloads, and how they line up with our onboarding lifecycle.

The Reliability pillar of Microsoft's Azure Well-Architected Framework starts from the assumption that components will fail, and asks whether the workload survives that gracefully. Its core principles: design for business requirements first, build in resilience and redundancy, plan for recovery with clear targets, keep the design as simple as reasonable, and — the one most relevant here — build in the monitoring and testing needed to know reliability actually holds up in practice, not just on paper.

Well-Architected principleWhere it shows up in our lifecycle
Design for business requirementsBusiness context and critical journeys are captured in Discovery (D1–D2), before any configuration starts.
Build in resilience and redundancyTopology and dependency mapping (D1) surfaces single points of failure worth flagging.
Plan for recovery, with targetsRecovery expectations belong in the same conversation as alert routing and ownership (D4).
Test that resilience holds upValidation (M5) exists specifically so reliability is demonstrated with evidence, not assumed.

Full detail: the Reliability pillar on Microsoft Learn ↗.

StandardsNaming, tagging & data-handling standardsThe baseline conventions applied during Deployment (P1) so search, ownership, and access scoping all work the same way across every service.

These are defaults to start from, not a fixed schema — confirm them against any existing convention already in use before a service's first deployment, and record the agreed variant in that service's implementation plan (D5).

StandardBaseline convention
Host / process group naming<env>-<region>-<service>-<role> — e.g. prod-eu-payments-api. Environment and region come first so lists sort into meaningful groups.
Required tagsowner (team, not a person), tier (criticality), cost-center, environment. Applied at deployment (P1) — see the field tip on tagging while you deploy.
Management zone scopingOne management zone per owning team, not per service — keeps access aligned to who's actually accountable without a zone per microservice.
Log masking defaultsMask by default; require an explicit, reviewed exception to ingest an unmasked field. Confirmed in Discovery (D3), applied in Deployment (P3).
Dashboard naming<service> — <audience> — e.g. Payments API — Support Team, so the intended audience is visible in every dashboard list.
  • A tag applied inconsistently is worse than no tag — one wrong value breaks every dashboard, filter, and access rule built on it.
  • These defaults exist so a Platform & Governance Lead doesn't have to reinvent the scheme for every new service — treat deviations as a decision to record, not a default to silently drift into.
Google SREBlameless postmortemsWhy the SRE model reviews failures by fixing systems, not finding someone to blame — and how that connects to the improvement backlog.

A blameless postmortem assumes everyone involved had good intentions and acted on the information they had at the time. The point isn't absolving anyone of responsibility — it's that blaming a person closes the investigation early, at "who," instead of continuing to "why did the system let this happen, and what would have caught it sooner." Teams that punish honest incident reports get less honest incident reports, not fewer incidents.

  • Write the postmortem for a validation gate the same way you'd write one for an incident — what was expected, what actually happened, and what closes the gap. The Field tips section runs on the same instinct.
  • A postmortem that keeps citing the same root cause release after release is really describing toil (see above) — raise it as a backlog item instead of writing the same postmortem again.
  • Full detail: the Postmortem Culture chapter of Google's SRE book ↗.
DORAThe four (now five) DORA metricsGoogle Cloud's DevOps Research and Assessment findings on what actually separates high-performing teams — independent of any specific vendor or platform.

DORA's multi-year research across tens of thousands of engineers found the same handful of metrics keep predicting team performance, regardless of tooling: deployment frequency and lead time for changes measure speed; change failure rate and time to restore service measure stability. The finding that surprised people: these don't trade off against each other. Elite performers are fast and stable, using the same disciplined practices — small changes, fast feedback, and good rollback paths — to get both.

  • This is a useful cross-check on the onboarding lifecycle itself: a slow, heavy M1–M6 process isn't automatically "more careful" — the DORA findings suggest speed and safety usually improve together, not at each other's expense.
  • Time to restore service is effectively MTTR by another name — it's worth tracking both onboarding lead time and post-handoff incident metrics on the same dashboard.
  • Full detail: the DORA metrics guide at dora.dev ↗ — vendor-neutral, run by Google Cloud's research team but not tied to any specific platform.

Milestone 1 — Intake
Onboarding

Confirm the application, its business priority, and the named contacts before any technical work is scheduled.

I1–I3Business priorityNamed stakeholders
Open milestone — Key Steps
1.1 Request & scope · Source: C4E onboarding process
I1 — Confirm the application and business priority

Prerequisites: An onboarding request has been raised by the application owner.

  1. Identify the application, its environments, and the business service it supports.
  2. Confirm business criticality and the tier assigned to the service.
  3. Record the reason for onboarding — new service, expanded coverage, or remediation.
💡 Criticality drives everything downstream — Tier determines coverage depth, alert routing, and how much validation the service needs. Getting it wrong here is expensive to correct after deployment.
✅ Validate: Application, environments, and business criticality recorded on the request.
I2 — Identify stakeholders and technical contacts
  1. Name the application owner accountable for the service.
  2. Identify technical contacts for application, infrastructure, and network.
  3. Identify the support team and escalation path that will receive alerts.
✅ Validate: Named owner and technical contacts recorded for every required area.
I3 — Accept the onboarding request and set target timing

Prerequisites: I1 and I2 complete.

  1. Confirm the requested scope is within the current platform and licensing envelope.
  2. Agree target timing with the application owner and the C4E.
  3. Accept the request into the onboarding queue.
✅ Validate: Onboarding request accepted; stakeholders named and target timing agreed.

M2
Milestone 2 — Readiness
Onboarding Platform & Governance

Validate access, architecture, connectivity, and test support so deployment is not blocked mid-flight.

R1–R4Access & identityReadiness decision
Open milestone — Key Steps
2.1 Access & prerequisites · Source: Platform & Governance
R1 — Validate platform access and role assignment

Prerequisites: Onboarding request accepted (I3).

  1. Confirm the required users exist through federated identity with MFA enforced.
  2. Assign least-privilege roles matched to what each person actually needs to do.
  3. Confirm management-zone or permission scoping for the service.
✅ Validate: Required users have working access at the correct permission scope.
Dynatrace Documentation: user management and permissions.
R2 — Confirm architecture, hosting, and connectivity prerequisites
  1. Confirm the hosting model — on-premises, Azure, AWS, GCP, or Kubernetes.
  2. Verify agent or collector connectivity across approved egress paths.
    • Confirm encrypted transport and any proxy requirements.
  3. Confirm supported technology versions for the components in scope.
💡 Check egress before deployment day — Blocked outbound paths are the most common cause of a stalled deployment. Confirming them during readiness turns a multi-day delay into a short conversation.
✅ Validate: Hosting model, connectivity path, and version support confirmed for everything in scope.
2.2 Scheduling & decision · Source: C4E onboarding process
R3 — Agree change windows and test support
  1. Agree the change windows available for instrumentation and restarts.
  2. Confirm who will generate or approve representative traffic for validation.
  3. Confirm the change-control records required before work begins.
✅ Validate: Change windows and test support confirmed in writing.
R4 — Record the readiness decision and open dependencies

Prerequisites: R1–R3 assessed.

  1. Assess readiness across business context, technical readiness, support readiness, and validation readiness.
  2. Record the decision: proceed, proceed with conditions, or hold.
  3. Log every unmet prerequisite as an open dependency with a named owner.
✅ Validate: Readiness decision recorded with an owner against each open dependency.

M3
Milestone 3 — Discovery
Onboarding Platform & Governance

Understand how the service actually works, then turn that into a documented implementation plan.

D1–D5Critical journeysImplementation plan
Open milestone — Key Steps
3.1 Service understanding · Source: C4E onboarding process
D1 — Review architecture and service dependencies

Prerequisites: Readiness decision recorded (R4).

  1. Collect existing architecture, context, and service-flow documentation.
  2. Run a short discovery session with the business owner, platform/infrastructure owners, and the ITSM lead — ask attendees to bring recent related incidents and current architecture or job-flow diagrams as pre-work.
  3. Confirm the components, integrations, and downstream dependencies in scope.
  4. Flag undocumented dependencies and assign an owner to each.
💡 A short session beats a long email thread — 60–90 minutes with the right five or six people in the room (business owner, app/platform owners, an infra rep, and ITSM) surfaces more than a week of back-and-forth over documentation.
✅ Validate: Architecture and dependency picture confirmed with the application owner.
D2 — Map critical user journeys
  1. Identify the journeys that matter most to the business.
  2. Trace each journey through the components that serve it.
  3. Agree which journeys warrant active validation through synthetic monitoring.
💡 Journeys beat component lists — A ranked set of critical journeys gives dashboards and alerts a purpose. Without it, coverage expands without improving anything anyone notices.
✅ Validate: Critical journeys documented and ranked with the application owner.
3.2 Telemetry and use cases · Source: Platform & Governance
D3 — Confirm required telemetry sources and integrations
  1. Confirm which signals are needed: metrics, traces, logs, real-user, synthetic, or batch/queue signals for scheduled and asynchronous work.
  2. Identify log sources and the fields that must be masked, excluded, or restricted.
  3. Confirm the ITSM, collaboration, and incident integrations required at handoff.
✅ Validate: Required telemetry sources and integrations agreed, including data-handling constraints.
D4 — Define operational use cases and alert expectations
  1. Define what the support team needs to detect, and how quickly.
  2. Agree what constitutes an actionable alert for this service.
  3. Confirm the routing destination and ownership for each alert class.
✅ Validate: Operational use cases and alert expectations agreed with the support team.
D5 — Produce the documented implementation plan

Prerequisites: D1–D4 complete.

  1. Translate the agreed scope into a component-level configuration plan.
  2. Sequence the work against the agreed change windows.
  3. Confirm the plan with the application owner and the C4E before deployment starts.
✅ Validate: Requirements and implementation plan documented and confirmed.

M4
Milestone 4 — Deployment
Onboarding Platform & Governance

Instrument, integrate, and configure the agreed capabilities in the target environment.

P1–P6InstrumentationDashboards & routing
Open milestone — Key Steps
4.1 Instrumentation · Source: Platform & Governance
P1 — Establish infrastructure coverage

Prerequisites: Implementation plan confirmed (D5).

  1. Deploy OneAgent across the hosts, clusters, or cloud resources in scope.
    • Follow the deployment method appropriate to the hosting model.
  2. Confirm each target reports host, process, and availability data.
  3. Apply the agreed naming, tagging, and management-zone metadata.
💡 Tag as you deploy — Metadata applied during deployment drives grouping, routing, and access scoping later. Retrofitting tags across an estate is far more work than applying them once.
✅ Validate: Required hosts, clusters, or cloud resources report reliably.
Dynatrace Documentation: OneAgent deployment.
P2 — Configure application services and trace ingestion
  1. Confirm service detection matches the understood architecture.
  2. Configure the trace ingestion approach agreed in Discovery — OneAgent or OpenTelemetry.
  3. Adjust service detection or naming rules where defaults misrepresent the service.
✅ Validate: Services and critical dependencies are detected and validated.
Dynatrace Documentation: service detection and OpenTelemetry ingestion.
P3 — Configure log ingestion and parsing
  1. Enable the log sources agreed in Discovery.
  2. Apply masking, exclusion, and retention rules before broad ingestion begins.
  3. Confirm logs correlate to the correct services and hosts.
✅ Validate: Required log sources are ingested, parsed, correlated, and governed.
4.2 Experience and signals · Source: Platform & Governance
P4 — Configure synthetic and real-user monitoring where approved

Prerequisites: Data-handling approval confirmed for real-user monitoring (D3).

  1. Configure synthetic monitors for the critical journeys agreed in D2.
  2. Enable real-user monitoring only where consent and data handling are understood.
  3. Confirm the resulting data is usable for the agreed journeys.
✅ Validate: Approved journeys produce usable real-user or synthetic data.
P5 — Build dashboards and alerting profiles
  1. Build the service view the support team will actually open during an incident.
  2. Configure alerting profiles matched to the use cases from D4.
  3. Set thresholds and sensitivity to the agreed expectations rather than defaults.
✅ Validate: Views and signals are usable, actionable, and owned.
P6 — Configure integrations and notification routing

Prerequisites: P1–P5 configured.

  1. Configure the ITSM, collaboration, or incident integrations agreed in D3.
  2. Route each alert class to the destination and owner agreed in D4.
  3. Send a test notification through every configured path.
✅ Validate: Agreed monitoring capabilities configured, with every notification path tested end to end.

M5
Milestone 5 — Validation
Onboarding Adoption & Outcomes

Confirm the data, topology, and signals are trustworthy and useful before ownership transfers.

V1–V5Signal qualityAcceptance evidence
Open milestone — Key Steps
5.1 Data and topology · Source: Adoption & Outcomes
V1 — Validate data quality and completeness

Prerequisites: Deployment complete (P6).

  1. Compare reported coverage against the scope agreed in Discovery.
  2. Identify gaps, duplicates, and components reporting no data.
  3. Confirm retention and data-handling rules are applied as agreed.
✅ Validate: Coverage matches agreed scope, with every gap logged and owned.
V2 — Validate topology and service detection
  1. Confirm key service relationships align with the understood architecture.
  2. Correct service detection or naming where the topology misrepresents reality.
  3. Confirm the business-service mapping resolves correctly.
✅ Validate: Key service relationships align with the understood architecture.
5.2 Signal usefulness · Source: Adoption & Outcomes
V3 — Validate dashboards and alert actionability
  1. Walk the support team through the dashboards during a working session.
  2. Review noisy and missed alerts against real conditions.
  3. Refine thresholds, sensitivity, and routing based on what the team reports.
💡 Validate with the people who get paged — An alert that reads clearly to whoever built it can still be unusable at 3am. The support team is the only reliable judge of actionability.
✅ Validate: Support team confirms dashboards and alerts are usable and correctly routed.
V4 — Confirm use-case coverage against Discovery
  1. Test each operational use case defined in D4 against the deployed configuration.
  2. Record expected versus actual behaviour for each case.
  3. Log any use case that cannot yet be satisfied, with the reason.
✅ Validate: Every agreed use case is either demonstrated or explicitly logged as outstanding.
V5 — Record acceptance evidence and tuning actions

Prerequisites: V1–V4 complete.

  1. Assemble the evidence pack: expected versus actual, with screenshots where behaviour matters.
  2. Record outstanding tuning actions with named owners and dates.
  3. Obtain the application owner’s written acceptance.
✅ Validate: Acceptance evidence captured and tuning actions recorded with owners.

Milestone 6 — Handoff
Onboarding Enablement & Learning Adoption & Outcomes

Transfer ownership with the knowledge, documentation, and improvement backlog needed to operate the service.

H1–H4Knowledge transferOptimization backlog
Open milestone — Key Steps
6.1 Ownership and support · Source: C4E onboarding process
H1 — Confirm ownership and the support model

Prerequisites: Acceptance recorded (V5).

  1. Confirm the ongoing owner for configuration, dashboards, and alerts.
  2. Confirm which support tier responds to each alert class.
  3. Confirm how often the service gets reviewed after handoff.
✅ Validate: Ongoing ownership and support responsibilities confirmed in writing.
H2 — Document runbooks, contacts, and escalation paths
  1. Document the response steps for each alert class.
  2. Record current contacts and escalation paths.
  3. Confirm the incident integration behaves as documented.
✅ Validate: Runbooks, contacts, escalation paths, and incident integrations are confirmed.
6.2 Enablement and improvement · Source: Enablement & Learning
H3 — Complete knowledge transfer and the enablement check
  1. Run the walkthrough for the owning and support teams.
  2. Confirm assigned users can perform the tasks their role requires.
  3. Point users at the learning path matched to their role.
💡 Demonstrated, not attended — Enablement closes when someone shows they can do the task, not when they appear on an attendance list.
✅ Validate: Assigned users demonstrate they can do the role.
H4 — Agree the optimization backlog and transition plan

Prerequisites: H1–H3 complete.

  1. Consolidate outstanding tuning actions and deferred scope into one backlog.
  2. Prioritise the backlog with the application owner.
  3. Confirm the transition into the stabilize, tune, and optimize rhythm.
✅ Validate: Support handoff confirmed; ownership transferred with a prioritised backlog.

Guide formats — Key Steps vs. Step-by-Step

This template ships one guide depth — Key Steps — embedded directly in the milestone browser above. Many practices add a second, deeper layer for less-familiar workstreams.

Key Steps

The ordered, gate-checked summary shown above — enough for someone who has delivered this workstream before. Every line item is a <details class="ks-item" id="li-CODE">.

Step-by-Step (optional)

Command-level, click-by-click depth for unfamiliar workstreams. If you add this layer, duplicate the Key Steps content using .sbs-item instead of .ks-item and do not repeat the id="li-CODE" — the canonical anchor stays on the Key Steps copy so search and cross-links have exactly one target per line item.

Example: what a Step-by-Step duplicate looks like
Key Steps (the official one, id="li-s1")

S1 — Creation of Project Team: identify roles, build the RACI table. ✅ Validate: RACI complete.

Step-by-Step (.sbs-item, no id)

S1 — Creation of Project Team: 1) Open the tracker template → Team tab. 2) Add each stakeholder's name, email, and role in columns A–D. 3) Set the RACI letter per milestone in columns E onward. 4) Share the tracker link in the kickoff invite. ✅ Validate: RACI complete.

Sizing & roadmap Draft

T-shirt estimates for complexity and duration per milestone, and a dynamic roadmap across the workstreams. Change any milestone's size and the schedule, lanes, and critical path recompute live.

Planning baseline, not a commercial estimate: sizes reflect a typical mid-size onboarding and exist for sequencing and staffing conversations — scope, environment count, and how often changes go through review can move them materially.
Sizing model
S

Small · ~1 week

Coordination-led work with few dependencies — logistics, scheduling, straightforward validation.

M

Medium · ~2 weeks

One workstream, known patterns, some back-and-forth with the application team — a contained build or validation cycle.

L

Large · ~3 weeks

Multi-step configuration with tuning loops, sign-off from the application owner, or change-control friction.

XL

Extra large · ~5 weeks

Multiple workstreams in parallel, external lead times, or estate-wide sweeps.

Adoption & alert-quality reporting

Definitions for the post-handoff review (see H4) — track these per service from M6 onward, not just at the moment of handoff.

MetricDefinition
Alert-to-incident ratioAlerts that led to a real, actioned incident, divided by total alerts fired. Low ratios point at noisy or badly-tuned alerting profiles.
False-positive rateAlerts closed with no underlying issue found, as a share of all alerts. Review anything trending up at the same cadence as alert tuning (V3).
Coverage by tierShare of onboarded services per tier (Tier 1, 2, 3) with dashboards and alerting profiles actually configured, not just planned.
Mean time to acknowledge (MTTA)Time from alert fired to someone acknowledging it — a leading indicator that complements MTTR.
Adoption check-in completionShare of services with a completed post-handoff check-in (see the CSM role) within the agreed review window.
